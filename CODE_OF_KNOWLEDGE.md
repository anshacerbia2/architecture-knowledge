# Code of Knowledge

## Epistemic discipline

Every important statement must be distinguishable as direct-source claim,
normalized-source claim, synthesis, inference, recommendation, hypothesis, or
opinion. Classification describes how a claim was produced; confidence
describes support strength. Neither is a lifecycle approval.

## Evidence hierarchy

Prefer standards and specifications, official security or government guidance,
original research, official product documentation, foundational books,
recognized industry publications, and then secondary educational material.
Authority is contextual: a vendor is authoritative about its product behavior
but not automatically about general architectural superiority.

## Source quality

Assess authority, proximity to the original specification, recency, stability,
scope fit, conflicts of interest, technical depth, reproducibility, peer review,
and maintenance status. Score dimensions independently. Do not collapse quality
to popularity or a single unexplained number.

## Synthesis rules

- Preserve qualifying conditions and source-specific scope.
- Separate descriptive claims from recommendations.
- State uncertainty and material assumptions.
- Document counterevidence and unresolved contradictions.
- Do not infer causality from association without evidence.
- Do not universalize results from a vendor, workload, or organization.
- Paraphrase; do not reproduce substantial source text.

## Architecture reasoning

Recommendations must connect context and constraints to alternatives, quality
attributes, trade-offs, risks, failure modes, and verification. A relationship
such as `improves` or `mitigates` is a claim and therefore needs conditions and
evidence.
